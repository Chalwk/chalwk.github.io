What is Autism?

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition that affects communication, social interaction, and behaviour. It's called a "spectrum" because autism affects people in different ways and to varying degrees.

Many autistic people and advocates prefer identity-first language ("autistic person") over person-first language ("person with autism"), because autism is not something separate from the person - it's part of how they experience the world. This guide uses identity-first language by default, while acknowledging that individual preferences vary.

Key facts about autism

  • Autism is not a disease or illness - it's a different way of experiencing the world.
  • Approximately 1 in 66 children in New Zealand are diagnosed with autism.
  • Autism is lifelong - people are born autistic and remain autistic throughout their lives.
  • There is no "cure" for autism, and many autistic people don't want one.
  • With appropriate support, autistic people can lead fulfilling lives.
  • In New Zealand, autism affects people of all ethnicities and backgrounds.
Early signs

Early signs of autism may include:

  • Limited eye contact
  • Delayed speech development
  • Repetitive behaviours
  • Difficulty with social interactions
  • Intense focus on specific interests
Diagnosis in NZ

In New Zealand, autism is typically diagnosed through:

  • Assessment by paediatricians or psychologists
  • Comprehensive diagnostic evaluation
  • Observation of behaviour
  • Parent/caregiver interviews
  • Referrals through your GP
Strengths

Many autistic people have remarkable strengths:

  • Attention to detail
  • Deep focus and concentration
  • Strong memory
  • Honesty and loyalty
  • Unique problem-solving abilities

Characteristics of Autism

Autism manifests differently in each individual, but common characteristics fall into these main categories:

Social communication
  • Difficulty with back-and-forth conversation
  • Challenges understanding nonverbal cues
  • Difficulty developing and maintaining relationships
  • May not initiate social interaction
  • May interpret language literally
Restricted / repetitive behaviours
  • Repetitive movements or speech
  • Insistence on sameness and routines
  • Highly restricted, fixated interests
  • Unusual responses to sensory input
  • Preoccupation with parts of objects
Sensory differences
  • Over or under-sensitivity to sounds, lights, textures
  • Unusual interest in sensory aspects of environment
  • Adverse response to specific sounds or textures
  • May seek or avoid certain sensory experiences
  • Can affect eating, dressing, and daily activities

What sensory experience can feel like

Sensory processing differences are one of the most misunderstood aspects of autism. Here are some examples of how the same environment can be experienced very differently:

Sensory overload

Imagine trying to focus while:

  • Fluorescent lights are flickering and buzzing
  • Multiple conversations are happening around you
  • Someone is wearing strong perfume
  • Your clothing tags are itching
  • A clock is ticking loudly

This overwhelming experience can make it difficult to concentrate or function.

Visual sensitivity

Imagine:

  • Bright lights causing physical discomfort or pain
  • Patterns that appear to move or vibrate
  • Difficulty filtering visual information
  • Being distracted by small visual details others don't notice

These visual sensitivities can make environments like shopping malls or classrooms challenging.

Auditory sensitivity

Imagine:

  • Every sound being equally loud and important
  • Difficulty filtering background noise from conversation
  • Certain frequencies causing physical pain
  • Being able to hear electricity humming or clocks ticking

This can make noisy environments overwhelming and conversations difficult to follow.

Myths vs Facts About Autism

There are many misconceptions about autism. Let's separate fact from fiction.

Myth

Vaccines cause autism.

This myth originated from a fraudulent study that has been thoroughly debunked. Extensive research has shown no link between vaccines and autism.

Fact

Autism has a strong genetic component.

Research indicates that autism is primarily genetic, though environmental factors may also play a role in some cases.

Myth

Autistic people lack empathy.

Many autistic people experience empathy deeply, though they may express it differently than neurotypical people.

Fact

Autism occurs across all racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic groups in NZ.

Autism affects people of all backgrounds in Aotearoa, though diagnosis rates may vary due to disparities in access to healthcare and cultural factors.

Myth

All autistic people have savant skills.

While some autistic people have exceptional skills in specific areas, this is not true for everyone on the spectrum.

Fact

Early intervention can improve outcomes.

While there is no cure, early diagnosis and appropriate support can help autistic people develop skills and reach their full potential.

Support Strategies in New Zealand

There are many ways to support autistic individuals in various settings in Aotearoa.

At home
  • Establish predictable routines
  • Create a calm, organised environment
  • Use visual schedules and timers
  • Provide advance notice of changes
  • Respect sensory needs and preferences
In school
  • Implement individual education plans (IEPs)
  • Provide clear, concise instructions
  • Offer breaks and quiet spaces
  • Use visual supports and social stories
  • Teach social skills explicitly
  • Access Ministry of Education support services
In the workplace
  • Provide clear job descriptions and expectations
  • Offer flexible work arrangements
  • Minimise sensory distractions
  • Use written communication when possible
  • Focus on strengths and talents
  • Access Work and Income support services

Community support
  • Autism New Zealand regional services
  • Altogether Autism
  • IHC advocacy services
  • Parent to Parent support networks

Communication tips

Try these strategies when communicating with autistic individuals:

Do
  • Be clear and direct
  • Allow processing time
  • Use visual supports
  • Respect personal space
Don't
  • Use sarcasm or irony
  • Force eye contact
  • Assume understanding
  • Take behaviours personally

Getting a diagnosis in New Zealand

The process for getting an autism diagnosis in New Zealand typically involves:

  1. Discuss your concerns with your GP (family doctor)
  2. Your GP may refer you to a paediatrician or psychologist
  3. Assessment by a multidisciplinary team may be recommended
  4. Diagnosis is based on observation, developmental history, and standardised assessment tools
  5. Once diagnosed, you can access support services through the Ministry of Health and Ministry of Education

Note: Wait times for publicly funded assessments can vary by region. Private assessments are also available.

Research & Reference Library

A curated collection of peer-reviewed research, official reports, and evidence-based resources for further reading. Where available, links point to open-access versions or publisher pages.

New Zealand research
  • The New Zealand Minds for Minds Autism Spectrum Disorder Self-Reported Cohort (Virues-Ortega et al., 2017) - the first national large-scale ASD research cohort in New Zealand, providing insights into diagnostic and sociodemographic trends.
  • Autism New Zealand - the national organisation provides support, resources, and information for autistic adults and their whānau across Aotearoa.
  • Altogether Autism - NZ information and advisory service, including a searchable research database and free personalised advice.
  • Te Puna Rangahau (University of Waikato Research Commons) - search the repository for NZ theses on autism, including adult diagnosis pathways and healthcare categorisation.
Key international research
  • Epidemiology of autism in adults across age groups and ability levels (Brugha et al., 2016) - British Journal of Psychiatry. A landmark study providing prevalence estimates for autism in adults of all ages and ability levels.
  • A meta-analysis of outcome studies of autistic adults - Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders. Quantifies quality of life and outcome measures across the adult autistic population.
  • Family support and empowerment: Post autism diagnosis support group for parents - Social Work with Groups. Measures the effect of peer support on parental advocacy and self-efficacy.
  • Sensory abnormality and quantitative autism traits in children with and without ASD - Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders. Examines the prevalence of sensory differences in the general population.
  • How do parents manage irritability, challenging behaviour, non-compliance and anxiety in children with ASD? - A meta-synthesis of parent-reported strategies.
  • A strength-based approach to parent education for children with autism - Journal of Positive Behavior Interventions. Evaluates an assets-focused model of parent coaching.
Handbooks & books
  • Handbook of Parent-Implemented Interventions for Very Young Children with Autism - Springer. Siller & Morgan (Eds). Comprehensive reference on parent-mediated intervention models.
  • Embedding Intervention Strategies within Everyday Family Routines (Kashinath & Yu, 2018) - A chapter in the Handbook, outlining research-based procedures for collaborating with families.
  • Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders - the primary peer-reviewed journal in the field; searchable open-access archive.
  • Autism CRC - Australia/NZ cooperative research centre; publishes systematic reviews, guidelines, and practitioner resources.
